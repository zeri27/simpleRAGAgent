First, all experiments were conducted using data generated from the GEMINI geothermal digital twin
simulator. While the simulator captures the thermodynamic and hydraulic interactions of a geothermal
direct-use system and enables controlled evaluation of different degradation mechanisms, it cannot fully
reproduce the complexity of real-world geothermal operations. Industrial geothermal data are affected by
various sources of variability that are not explicitly represented in the benchmark, including sensor drift,
calibration errors, communication failures, missing measurements, changing control strategies, mainte-
nance practices, and site-specific operational procedures. Consequently, the reported results should be
interpreted as evidence of relative model performance under controlled geothermal operating conditions
rather than definitive evidence of real-world deployment performance.

Second, the benchmark represents a single geothermal digital-twin environment with fixed underlying
reservoir properties and system assumptions. Although multiple degradation mechanisms, operational
interventions, and forecasting tasks are considered, all datasets originate from the same simulation frame-
work and therefore share a common physical model. As a result, the study evaluates transfer to previ-
ously unseen operating conditions within the benchmark environment rather than transfer across multiple
geothermal assets, reservoirs, or geological settings. The presented results should therefore not be inter-
preted as direct evidence of cross-field or cross-reservoir generalization. Demonstrating such capabilities
would require validation across geothermal systems with substantially different reservoir properties, fluid
characteristics, operational strategies, and geological conditions.

Third, the degradation processes considered in this work are intentionally simplified representations of
complex physical phenomena. Heat exchanger fouling, ESP degradation, and filter clogging are modeled
through controlled parameter changes that reproduce plausible degradation trajectories and maintenance
cycles. However, real degradation mechanisms are often driven by interacting physical, chemical, and op-
erational processes that may evolve differently across assets. For example, scaling and fouling behaviour
depend on fluid chemistry, temperature gradients, flow conditions, and maintenance history, while ESP
degradation may be influenced by electrical, hydraulic, and mechanical failure modes occurring simul-
taneously. The simplified degradation models therefore provide a useful benchmarking framework but
cannot fully represent the diversity of faults encountered in operational geothermal facilities.

Fourth, the study evaluates foundation models exclusively in a zero-shot setting. This choice was de-
liberate, as the primary objective was to assess how effectively pretrained TSFMs transfer to a geothermal
monitoring task without task-specific adaptation. However, previous studies have shown that fine-tuning,
parameter-efficient adaptation, and few-shot learning can substantially improve downstream performance,
particularly in domains characterized by strong covariate dependencies. The results presented here should
therefore be viewed as a lower-bound estimate of achievable performance rather than as the maximum
capability of the evaluated models.

Fifth, uncertainty treatment remains limited. Forecast uncertainty is represented through prediction
intervals generated by the forecasting models and is subsequently used within the novelty-detection frame-
work. However, the study does not distinguish between different sources of uncertainty, including mea-
surement uncertainty, model uncertainty, operational uncertainty, and subsurface geological uncertainty.
Such uncertainty sources play a central role in geothermal operations and may affect both forecasting
reliability and anomaly-detection performance. Future work should investigate uncertainty calibration,
probabilistic forecasting metrics, ensemble approaches, and physics-guided uncertainty quantification to
provide a more complete assessment of model trustworthiness.

Sixth, the anomaly-detection experiments investigate only three degradation mechanisms and rely
on synthetic labels that are directly available from the simulator. Although these faults were selected
because they represent important operational challenges in geothermal systems, they do not span the full
spectrum of failure modes encountered in practice. Industrial deployments often experience overlapping faults, evolving degradation mechanisms, unknown fault classes, and operational events that are difficult
to distinguish from true equipment degradation. Evaluating TSFMs under such conditions remains an
important open research direction.

Finally, while inference runtimes are reported for all evaluated models, this work does not investi-
gate how computational requirements scale with increasing system complexity, larger sensor networks, or
multi-asset deployment scenarios. Practical deployment in geothermal operations may require simulta-
neous monitoring of hundreds of variables across multiple assets. Understanding the trade-offs between
forecasting accuracy, computational efficiency, model maintainability, and deployment complexity there-
fore remains an important area for future research.

Taken together, these limitations highlight that the primary contribution of this work is the systematic
evaluation of pretrained time-series foundation models within a controlled geothermal benchmarking
environment. Validation on real geothermal field data, multiple reservoir settings, and broader classes of
operational degradation remains necessary before stronger conclusions regarding operational deployment
and large-scale generalization can be drawn
